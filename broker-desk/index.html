House rules

Non-negotiable
ND + MN

If it isn't in writing the same day, it didn't happen

Every price change, every extension, every "the seller said it's fine," every instruction from a client goes into the file the day it happens — email confirmation to the other side, note in the transaction record. Verbal is how you remember it; written is how it gets proven.

Nearly every complaint that reaches a real estate commission is a disagreement about what somebody was told and when. The agent who wrote a two-line confirming email wins that argument in about four minutes. The agent who didn't spends nine months explaining their memory.

  • Any sentence starting "I told them, but…"
  • Deadline extensions agreed by text and never amended into the contract
ND + MN

We don't practice law, appraise, or inspect

We explain what a contract term does in plain language. We do not tell a client what a contract means for their rights, draft custom legal language, opine on title defects, or interpret a will, divorce decree, or trust. That's an attorney's job and we hand it off by name.

The temptation is worst on the easy ones — a quitclaim between siblings, a contract for deed someone found online. Those are exactly the deals that come back. The referral costs you nothing and buys you the entire defense.

North Dakota has an express rule against licensees giving legal advice. Both states hold you to disclosure of material facts you know, which is a different duty from opining on law.
NDAC 70-02-03-08 NDAC 70-02-03-09
ND + MN

Know which state you're standing in before you open your mouth

Fargo–Moorhead makes this a daily problem. The agency disclosure, the listing form requirements, the earnest money clock, the title process, and the seller disclosure obligations all differ across the river. Establish the property's state first, then pull the right form set and the right timeline.

The most common cross-border mistake isn't legal — it's the closing. Agents run a Moorhead deal on a Fargo mental model, forget the well disclosure certificate or the Torrens question, and lose a week they promised the buyer.

  • Using an ND form set on an MN property or vice versa
  • Quoting an earnest money deadline from the wrong state (24 hours vs. 3 business days)
  • Assuming an ND-style attorney title opinion applies to an MN closing
ND + MN

The file is the defense

A complete file has: signed agency disclosure, signed representation agreement, the disclosure forms with both signatures, every amendment in order, the earnest money trail, and a dated activity log. Complete it as you go. A file assembled after a complaint arrives looks exactly like a file assembled after a complaint arrives.

North Dakota requires true copies of the agency disclosure retained in the broker's file, and requires the brokerage to retain a signed copy of the seller's property disclosure from both seller and buyer after closing.
NDAC 70-02-03-15.1 NDCC 47-10-02.1

ND + MN

The clock starts at final acceptance, not at signing

"He signed Tuesday" is not the answer to when the clock started. What starts it is the moment the last party signed and that acceptance was communicated back to the other side. A seller who signed at 4pm Tuesday but whose agent didn't deliver it until Wednesday morning has, in practical terms, a Wednesday deal — and if the two of you disagree about which, you have a dispute about every downstream date.

Fix this at the moment of acceptance, every time: write the date of final acceptance on the face of the agreement where the form provides for it, and send a one-line email to the other agent confirming it. Thirty seconds of work that eliminates the single most common deadline argument in the business.

  • Signature pages returned without a date, or with the date left to the notary
  • Acceptance signed but sitting unsent overnight
  • Two different acceptance dates written on two different counterparts
ND + MN

Every counteroffer resets everything

A counteroffer is a rejection of the prior offer plus a new offer. When it's accepted, that acceptance date is the one every deadline counts from — not the date of the original offer. Three rounds of counters means the deadlines you calculated on round one are all wrong.

Recalculate after the final counter, not before. And watch the expiration on your own counter: an offer with a stated deadline dies at that deadline, and a "yes" that arrives afterward is a new offer that your client is free to decline — which is occasionally very useful and occasionally a disaster, depending on which side you're on.

  • Deadlines carried forward from the original offer into the executed contract
  • A counter accepted after its own expiration with nobody noticing
ND + MN

How to count the days

Both states use the same base rule: exclude the first day, include the last. So a 10-day inspection period on a contract accepted the 3rd runs through the 13th, not the 12th. Where they diverge is the last day.

Minnesota — if the last day is a Saturday, Sunday, or legal holiday, that day is omitted from the computation. North Dakota — the last day is excluded if it's a holiday, and every Sunday is a holiday; Saturday is not separately enumerated. Read the contract too: many forms define "days" as calendar days and set their own weekend rule, and the contract's definition controls.
Minn. Stat. § 645.15 NDCC 1-02-15 NDCC 1-03-01, 1-03-02

Never let a deadline ride to its last day anyway. If the inspection response is due Friday, have it in Wednesday. The statutory rule is what you argue about after something went wrong — it is not a schedule.

ND + MN

Earnest money: two states, two clocks

These are the deadlines agents miss most, because they run from different events in each state and neither one waits for you to get back to the office.

North Dakota: deposit within 24 hours of receipt by the broker or salesperson, unless the purchase contract provides otherwise; if received the day before a holiday or a day the depository is closed, it goes in the next business day. Minnesota: if the written agreement is silent, the listing broker deposits within three business days of receiving the money or of final acceptance, whichever is later; on a rejected offer the money goes back no later than the next business day.
NDAC 70-02-01-15 Minn. Stat. § 82.75
  • ND: the clock runs from receipt, so it can start before the contract is even fully executed
  • MN: "whichever is later" cuts both ways — money held before acceptance doesn't start the clock, but acceptance does
MN

The 72-hour protective list clock

Counted from the expiration of the agreement, not from the last showing and not from when you remembered. Miss it and the override clause is unenforceable, on both listing agreements and buyer's broker agreements.

Calendar it the day you take the listing, dated to expiration, with a reminder the day before. This is the only deadline in the business that expires while nothing is happening — no closing, no inspection, no phone ringing to remind you.

Related timing rules on the same forms: a definite expiration date is required, and holdover clauses, automatic extensions, and override clauses beyond six months are prohibited (two years for business sales).
Minn. Stat. § 82.66 subd. 1–2

Who has to sign

Authority & capacity
ND + MN

Married sellers: both spouses sign the homestead, even if only one is on title

This is the signature question that kills closings, and it surprises people every time. In both states, a homestead owned by a married person cannot be validly conveyed on one spouse's signature alone — the non-titled spouse has homestead rights that have to be signed away regardless of whose name is on the deed.

Minnesota: if the owner is married, no conveyance of the homestead is valid without the signatures of both spouses, subject to narrow exceptions (purchase-money mortgage, spouse-to-spouse conveyance, joint tenancy severance). An attorney-in-fact may sign for a spouse. North Dakota: both spouses must execute and acknowledge the same instrument before an officer authorized to take acknowledgments.
Minn. Stat. § 507.02 NDCC 47-18-05

Ask "are you married?" at the listing appointment, not at closing — and ask it even when the tax record shows one name. The hard version is a separated-but-not-divorced seller who does not want their spouse involved. That is a lawyer's problem and you should say so on day one, because it does not get easier at day forty.

  • Tax record shows one owner and the seller casually mentions a spouse
  • Divorce in progress with no decree yet
  • ND: spouses signing separate counterparts rather than the same instrument
ND + MN

It has to be in writing and signed by the party being held to it

Both states' statute of frauds voids an unwritten agreement for the sale of land or a lease over one year. Minnesota requires the writing to express the consideration and be subscribed by the party by whom the sale is to be made, or their agent authorized in writing. North Dakota requires it be subscribed by the party to be charged — and if signed by an agent, the agent's authority must itself be in writing subscribed by that party.

The practical consequence: an agent cannot sign a purchase agreement or listing for a client on a verbal go-ahead. Written authority first.
Minn. Stat. § 513.05 NDCC 9-06-04

The flip side matters just as much: emails and texts can satisfy a statute of frauds if they contain the terms and a signature the law recognizes. Be careful what you type. "Sounds good, we'll take $310" from a seller's own email account is not obviously harmless.

ND + MN

Entities, trusts, estates, and powers of attorney

For anything other than an individual owner, the question is always the same: who has authority, and what document proves it? Get that document early — it is routinely the thing that delays closing.

  • LLC or corporation — who is authorized to sign, and is there an operating agreement or resolution proving it? Is the entity in good standing with the Secretary of State?
  • Trust — the trustee signs, and the closer will want a certificate of trust or the trust instrument. Confirm the property was actually deeded into the trust.
  • Estate — a personal representative must actually be appointed. "We're the kids" is not authority.
  • Power of attorney — must cover real property, must be in effect, and must not have terminated on the principal's death. Send it to the closer for review before you write.
  • Multiple owners — all of them sign, including the one in Arizona and the one nobody has spoken to since the funeral.

Ask for the authority document the week you list, not the week you close. If someone gets defensive about producing it, that is information — it usually means the authority is contested, and you would much rather learn that before a buyer has spent money on an inspection.

ND + MN

Electronic signatures are fine — if the parties agreed to go electronic

Both states have adopted the Uniform Electronic Transactions Act, so an electronic signature on an electronic record satisfies a signature requirement, including for the statute of frauds. What UETA does not do is relax the underlying requirements: if a document must be in writing and signed, it still must be — electronic means just count as writing and signing. It also depends on the parties having agreed to conduct the transaction electronically.

North Dakota adopted UETA in 2001; it applies to electronic records and signatures for transactions conducted after July 31, 2001, and expressly does not reach wills, codicils, or testamentary trusts, or most of the Uniform Commercial Code. Notarized and acknowledged documents are a separate question with their own rules — the ND homestead requirement to acknowledge before an authorized officer is not satisfied by a click.
Minn. Stat. ch. 325L NDCC ch. 9-16 NDCC 9-16-01
  • A signer who says they'd rather sign on paper — honor it, and don't mix media on one document
  • Assuming e-signature covers the deed and the acknowledgment; ask the closer
ND + MN

Initials, amendments, and the version that actually governs

Every handwritten change gets initialed and dated by every party. Every post-execution change happens on an amendment, signed by everyone, referencing the original by date and property. Number your amendments. The file should make it obvious, to a stranger, which version governs.

When you have four amendments and someone asks what the closing date is, the honest answer should take you five seconds. If it takes five minutes, the file is the problem, and you'll find that out at the worst possible time.

  • An amendment signed by the buyer but never returned to the seller for signature
  • Changes made on one party's counterpart only
  • An extension agreed by email that never became an amendment

When the contract is silent

Default rules & gap-fillers
ND + MN

First question: is it a gap, or is it a defect?

A missing term is one of two very different problems. A gap is something the parties simply didn't address — who holds the earnest money, when it's deposited — and the statute or the common law supplies an answer. A defect is a term the law requires the document to contain. That one isn't filled in by a default rule; it's a compliance problem, and it gets fixed by amendment now, not explained away later.

Sort it into the right bucket before you do anything else. Agents burn a lot of anxiety trying to reason out a default rule for something that was actually mandatory, and the answer to mandatory is never "well, what would be reasonable." It's "get it signed this week."

MNND

The listing has no expiration date

This is a defect, not a gap. Minnesota requires a listing agreement to contain a definite expiration date, and separately prohibits holdover clauses and automatic extensions — so there is no reading of an open-ended Minnesota listing that comes out fine. North Dakota's rules require listing contracts to include the commission amount and govern listings directly; an ND listing with no definite term is likewise not something to leave sitting.

The fix, both states: a signed amendment adding a definite expiration date, referencing the original listing by date and property, signed by every party who signed the original. Date the amendment the day it's actually signed. Do not backdate it, do not reprint the original with the date filled in, and do not write it on the original.
Minn. Stat. § 82.66 subd. 1(b) NDAC 70-02-03-04, -05

Worked example. A seller signs a listing on 7/15 on a property in Crookston — Polk County, so this is a Minnesota listing and § 82.66 governs. No expiration date anywhere in the document. You don't get to infer a reasonable term, and you don't get to treat it as running until someone cancels. Call the seller, explain it plainly as a paperwork correction on our end rather than anything they did, and get an amendment signed setting the expiration. If the seller won't sign one, you have an unenforceable listing and a decision to make about whether to keep marketing it — take that to the broker the same day.

  • The instinct to quietly fix the original document — that turns a compliance issue into a falsified-document issue
  • An expiration date that exists but is blank, or says "until sold"
  • Discovering this at offer time, when the seller now has leverage to renegotiate your fee
ND + MN

The contract says nothing about when earnest money gets deposited

This one is a true gap, and both states fill it. Minnesota's rule is written for exactly this situation — it applies "if the written agreement between the parties is silent." North Dakota's runs from receipt unless the purchase contract provides otherwise, which means silence gets you the 24-hour rule.

ND: 24 hours from receipt by the broker or salesperson; next business day if the depository is closed. MN: three business days from receipt or final acceptance, whichever is later.
NDAC 70-02-01-15 Minn. Stat. § 82.75
MN

The contract doesn't say who holds the earnest money

Minnesota supplies the answer: unless the parties agree otherwise in writing, the broker with whom trust funds are deposited is the listing broker. So silence means the listing side holds it, and a verbal arrangement to the contrary is not the exception — the exception has to be written.

Minn. Stat. § 82.75
ND

ND: the earnest money contract has no forfeiture-division provision

North Dakota requires the earnest money contract to include a separate written provision, approved by all parties including the broker, covering how the money gets divided if the transaction doesn't close and the money is kept as a forfeiture payment. If it isn't in there, that's a defect to fix on the front end — not a question to work out after a deal collapses, when nobody is feeling reasonable.

NDAC 70-02-01-15

Disputed earnest money with no written division provision is the single most reliable way to end up holding money you can't release to anyone without risk. Never split it on your own judgment of fairness — that's a broker call, and often a lawyer's.

ND

ND: there's no written agency agreement at all

North Dakota supplies a default and it may not be the one you assumed: in all real estate transactions, the licensee is the agent of the seller unless all parties otherwise agree in writing. So an agent who has been informally "helping a buyer" without paperwork has not created buyer agency by being helpful — and has meanwhile skipped a required disclosure.

The agency disclosure is separately required at first substantive contact, as its own signed document, with a true copy in the broker's file.
NDAC 70-02-03-15.1 NDCC ch. 43-23
ND + MN

Nobody wrote down who pays for title evidence

There's no statutory default here — it's allocated by contract and by local custom, and custom varies by county on both sides of the river. In North Dakota the cost in question is usually the abstract update plus the attorney's title opinion; in Minnesota it depends on whether the property is abstract or Torrens and what the title company needs.

Because there's no default, silence here means an argument at closing, and it's an argument neither party is wrong in. Settle it in the purchase agreement every single time. If you're on a rural ND property and nobody knows where the abstract is, raise it in week one — that's a schedule problem before it's a cost problem.

ND + MN

Fixtures and personal property weren't listed

The general rule is that fixtures — things attached to the property — convey, and personal property doesn't. The trouble is that the interesting items sit right on the line: the mounted television, the washer and dryer, the shed on skids, the mineral rights, the propane in the tank, the playset, the mirrors, the window coverings.

Never let this be resolved by the general rule. It costs three minutes at the listing appointment to write down what stays and what goes, and every disagreement about a wall-mounted TV is really a disagreement about trust, arriving on the day of the walkthrough when you can least afford one.

  • Mineral rights on any ND property with acreage — ask, and get it in writing
  • Appliances shown in listing photos that the seller intends to take
ND + MN

When you genuinely can't find the answer

Work the ladder in order and stop as soon as you have it. Most questions die at step two.

  • 1. Read the document again, all of it. The term is somewhere else in the form more often than anyone admits — in the addenda, in a definitions paragraph, or on a page nobody scrolled to.
  • 2. Check this page and the statute. Determine whether you're looking at a gap with a default rule or a required term that's missing.
  • 3. Ask the designated broker. Same day. This is the step that exists so you don't have to be certain on your own.
  • 4. Ask the closer or title company. They see hundreds of these and they'll tell you what actually happens at the table in your county.
  • 5. Ask the state association's legal hotline if the brokerage has access — that's what it's for.
  • 6. Call the regulator — or engage counsel if the money or the exposure is real.
North Dakota Real Estate Commission — 701-328-9749, ndrec@nd.gov, 1120 College Dr Ste 204, Bismarck ND 58501. Minnesota Department of Commerce, licensing — 651-539-1599 or 877-876-4430, licensing.commerce@state.mn.us; general line 651-539-1600 or 800-657-3602.
ND REC contact MN Commerce — real estate

On making that call, since nobody enjoys it: ask the general version of your question, not your fact pattern. "How does the definite-expiration-date requirement work if a listing was signed without one?" gets you a helpful two-minute answer. "My seller signed on the 15th and I need to know if I still have a listing" asks them for a ruling on your file, which they can't give and which makes the call longer and more uncomfortable for everyone. You're asking how a rule reads. Nobody has ever been disciplined for asking how a rule reads.

ND + MN

The three orderings that are not negotiable

Almost everything in a file can be done out of order and fixed later. These three cannot, because doing them late doesn't create an inconvenience — it creates a violation that already happened.

1. Agency disclosure before the engagement. First substantive contact, before the listing agreement or buyer's broker agreement is signed. 2. Signed engagement before you act. No sign, photo, or "coming soon" before the listing is signed; no touring before the buyer agreement is signed. 3. Seller disclosure before final acceptance. Both states put it before signing, not before closing.
NDAC 70-02-03-15.1 Minn. Stat. § 82.66 Minn. Stat. § 513.55 NDCC 47-10-02.1

A backdated document is worse than a late one, every time. If you discover one of these happened out of order, the fix is to sign it now with today's date and a short written note explaining the correction — then tell the broker. That's a bad afternoon. Backdating is a license case.

ND + MN

Front-load the slow things

Most deals that die on time died because something with a long lead time was started late. The fast items feel urgent and the slow items are invisible until they're critical.

Start in week one, every time: abstract location and update on any ND property; well and septic paperwork and any county compliance inspection; pending assessment search; flood determination and an actual insurance quote; authority documents for any entity, trust, or estate; payoff figures where a short sale is possible; estoppels and deposit ledgers on occupied rentals; survey where the legal description is uncertain.

A lost abstract on a rural North Dakota property is the reigning champion here — it can add weeks and there is no way to hurry it once you're behind. Ask "where is the abstract?" at the listing appointment. Nobody ever regrets that question.

Agency & representation

Who you work for
ND

North Dakota: disclose at first substantive contact, on a separate signed document

The disclosure comes before the listing agreement with a seller, before the buyer's broker agreement with a buyer, and before you discuss personal financial information or start negotiating with anyone else. It has to be its own document — not a paragraph inside the listing — offered for signature, with a true copy in the broker's file.

"First substantive contact" is earlier than most agents think. It is not the showing. It's the moment the conversation stops being "yes, that one's still available" and starts being "what could you get for it" or "what can you afford." Hand the form over at the point you'd naturally start giving advice.

Absent a written agreement otherwise, the licensee is the seller's agent. Brokerage firms providing services under a written agency agreement owe loyalty, obedience, disclosure, confidentiality, reasonable care, diligence, and accounting. Violations are disciplinable.
NDAC 70-02-03-15.1 NDAC 70-02-03-15, -15.2 NDCC ch. 43-23
  • Disclosure buried in the listing packet instead of standing alone
  • Open house conversations that turn substantive with no form in hand
MN

Minnesota: four options on the form, and you pick one on purpose

At first substantive contact in a residential transaction you deliver the agency disclosure form. It lays out four roles: seller's broker, buyer's broker, dual agent, and facilitator. The form is a disclosure of options, not the representation agreement itself — you still need the signed listing or buyer's broker agreement to actually represent anyone.

Agents treat this like a receipt to be collected. Treat it like a two-minute conversation instead: "Here are the four ways this can work, here's the one I'm proposing, here's what changes if we end up on both sides." Clients who understand the map don't file complaints when they end up somewhere on it.

Compliance with the statutory disclosure satisfies the common-law obligation to disclose agency relationships. A facilitator owes confidentiality and legally required duties only — not fiduciary representation — unless a written facilitator services agreement says more.
Minn. Stat. § 82.67
ND + MN

Dual agency: the honest version

Dual agency means one brokerage — or two agents licensed to the same broker — represents both sides. Both states allow it with informed written consent. What it actually does is strip out advocacy: you cannot argue price for either party, cannot advise either one on strategy against the other, and cannot leak the other's motivation or willingness to move.

TruHome's default is to avoid it. Not because it's improper — it's legal and sometimes it's genuinely the client's best option — but because the two things clients hire us for, negotiation and candid advice, are the two things dual agency removes. When it does come up, take it to the broker before you take it to the client, and price the conversation honestly: "I can carry this to closing, but I can't push for either of you on price."

Minnesota requires written informed consent from all parties in a specified boxed format, and the consent goes in the purchase agreement. Confidential information about price, terms, and motivation stays confidential unless a party instructs disclosure in writing.
Minn. Stat. § 82.67 subd. 3–4 NDAC 70-02-03-15.1
  • Consent collected after the offer is already negotiated
  • An agent "just passing along" one side's bottom line to the other
ND

Confidentiality doesn't expire when the deal does

You may not disclose a party's willingness to pay more or accept less, their motivation, or their willingness to accept different financing terms — and that obligation continues after the representation ends, expires, or completes. It lifts only on written consent, legal compulsion, or when the information becomes public from another source.

This one catches good agents years later. A past seller's divorce, a past buyer's stretched budget — that's still sealed. If you can't remember whether you learned something as their agent or at a barbecue, treat it as sealed.

The confidentiality rules never permit hiding material defects in the property you know about, or anything that would be fraudulent misrepresentation if withheld.
NDAC 70-02-03-15.1
ND

Designated broker and appointed agents

North Dakota's rules address the designated broker's supervisory role and the appointment of agents within a firm. When two TruHome agents end up on opposite sides, that's a brokerage-level decision about appointment and consent — not something the two agents negotiate between themselves.

Escalate in-house both-sides situations to the designated broker before either agent writes anything.
NDAC 70-02-03-17

The listing appointment

Winning the business
ND + MN

Forty-five minutes of prep wins most listings before you arrive

Before the appointment: pull the county assessor record and tax history, pull the last sale and any prior listing history including expireds and withdrawals, drive the block, pull three genuine comparables and two near-misses, check for permits and any assessment, and find out who owns the mortgage if you can. Know the property better than the seller expects you to.

The listing is usually won in the first ten minutes, and it's won on specificity. "Your neighbors at 1412 closed in March at $312 and took a $6,000 concession" beats any brochure about our marketing. Sellers hire the person who already did work for free.

  • A prior expired listing nobody asked about — find out why it failed before you repeat it
  • Special assessments in newer developments that the seller has forgotten about
ND + MN

The appointment arc

Tour first and let them talk. Then sit at the kitchen table and ask why they're moving and when they need to be out — before any number comes up. Then the market, then the price range, then how we get there, then the paperwork. Agency disclosure comes out at the front of that sequence, not the back.

Agents lose listings by presenting instead of asking. If you've been talking for more than four minutes straight, you've stopped selling. And never present price before you understand motivation — the same number is good news to a seller relocating in sixty days and an insult to one who's "just seeing what it'd bring."

"Before we talk about what it's worth, tell me what you need this sale to do for you. If it doesn't do that, the number doesn't matter."

ND + MN

Never quote a price on the phone

When a caller asks "what's my house worth," the answer is a range plus an appointment: the honest reason is that condition and updates move the number more than square footage does, and you can't see either over the phone.

A phone number is a number you'll have to defend or retreat from, and both are bad. You'll either be too high — and now every real opinion sounds like a bait and switch — or too low, and you're out before you started.

"Houses like yours in that area have been closing between $285 and $320. Where you land in that range is almost entirely condition and updates, and I'd be guessing until I see it. I can be there Thursday at 5:30 or Saturday morning."

ND + MN

Find the real timeline and the real floor

Two questions decide how the whole listing runs: when do they need to be out, and what's the lowest number that still works for them. You need the mortgage payoff, whether there's a second, whether a new purchase is contingent on this one, and whether anyone else has to sign.

The "anyone else has to sign" question is the one agents skip and regret. Estranged spouse still on title, an heir in another state, a co-signing parent, a contract for deed nobody mentioned. Find it at the table, not three days before closing.

  • Seller owes more than the realistic sale price — that's a short sale conversation, escalate it
  • Title held in a trust or an estate with no personal representative appointed yet
  • A divorce decree that assigns the house but no one has recorded anything
ND + MN

No sign, no photos, no marketing before signatures

Minnesota is explicit: you need a signed listing agreement or other signed written authorization before advertising the property, including putting a sign in the yard. North Dakota has its own rules on listings and for-sale signs. Treat the rule as universal — nothing goes public until the file has signatures.

MN: signed listing or written authorization required before any advertising, sign included. ND: separate rules govern listings and for-sale signs.
Minn. Stat. § 82.66 subd. 1 NDAC 70-02-03-04, -10
  • "Coming soon" social posts before the agreement is executed
  • Photographer scheduled and sign ordered off a verbal yes

The number

How we build a CMA

Three closed sales inside six months and inside a mile where the market supports it, adjusted for condition, finished square footage, garage stalls, and lot. Then the active competition — what a buyer can choose instead of this house today. Then the expireds, because they tell you where the ceiling actually is. Adjust for what a buyer would pay, not what an improvement cost.

Two adjustments people get wrong in this market: finished basements never return what they cost, and the garage matters more here than almost anywhere. In our winters a third stall or a heated shop moves the number in a way a coastal comp model would never predict.

  • Comps from a different school district or across a highway — adjacent on a map, different market
  • New construction comps used against a resale, or vice versa, without a builder-incentive adjustment
  • Pulling comps to justify a number the seller already said out loud
ND + MN

The pricing conversation

Give the range, then the recommendation, then what each price does. Show days-on-market by price band. Tell them what happens at each: priced right, showings in the first ten days and an offer in three weeks; priced high, no showings, a stale listing, and a lower net in ninety days than they'd have gotten in three.

Never argue about the number. Argue about the plan. A seller who insists on high can have it — with a written price-reduction schedule signed at the same table. That converts a fight into a calendar, and the calendar wins in three weeks without either of you losing face.

"I'll list it at your number. What I want in exchange is this: if we don't have an offer by day 21, we go to $X, and if we don't have one by day 45, we go to $Y. Let's sign that now so neither of us has to have this conversation again when you're stressed."

ND + MN

The price reduction ladder

Showings but no offers is a condition or a competition problem. No showings at all is a price problem, full stop, and no amount of marketing fixes it. Reductions should be big enough to cross a search-filter boundary — $304,900 to $299,900 opens a new pool of buyers; $304,900 to $302,500 opens nothing and burns a reduction.

Two meaningful cuts beat five cosmetic ones. A listing with a long trail of small reductions reads as desperate to every buyer's agent who pulls the history, and it invites lowballs. Cut once, cut properly, and let it work.

Disclosure

Say the bad thing
MN

Minnesota seller disclosure: broad, and it bites for two years

Before signing an agreement to sell residential real property, the seller must make a written disclosure of all material facts they're aware of that could adversely and significantly affect an ordinary buyer's use and enjoyment, or any intended use the seller knows about. Good faith, best of their knowledge, at the time of disclosure. Delivering it to the buyer's licensee counts as delivering it to the buyer.

A seller who fails to disclose while aware of material facts is liable: the buyer can sue for damages and equitable relief, and the action must be commenced within two years after closing. Fraud and negligent misrepresentation claims survive alongside it.
Minn. Stat. § 513.55 Minn. Stat. § 513.57

Coach sellers toward over-disclosure and mean it. A disclosed, repaired, documented problem costs a price adjustment. An undisclosed one costs the sale price back plus fees, two years later, when they've already spent it.

North Dakota seller disclosure: required, with a list of exemptions

For residential property of no more than four units, the seller must make a written disclosure of material facts before the parties sign final acceptance of the purchase agreement — latent defects, general condition, environmental, structural, and mechanical. When a licensee is involved the brokerage must retain a signed copy from both seller and buyer after closing, and the Commission can discipline licensees for violations.

Exempt: court-ordered sales, government agency transactions, foreclosures and related lender sales, fiduciary sales (estates, guardianships, trusts), sales between co-owners or family members, and newly constructed unoccupied residential property.

Exempt is not the same as safe. An estate sale is exempt from the form and fully exposed to a fraud claim if the personal representative knew about the wet basement and said nothing. When a seller is exempt, our advice is still to disclose.

ND + MN

What you know, you disclose — confidentiality never covers defects

Client confidentiality protects motivation, price flexibility, and financing willingness. It has never protected a material defect. If you know the sump has run continuously since April, that goes to the buyer regardless of what your seller prefers.

If a seller instructs you to withhold a defect, you have a decision to make that afternoon, not eventually. Put the advice in writing, give them the chance to correct course, and if they won't, terminate the listing. A commission is not worth a license, and the deal is going to fail anyway — just later and more expensively.

NDAC 70-02-03-15.1

Signed buyer agreement before you tour anything

ND + MN

The compensation conversation, said plainly

Cover four things before they sign: what you'll be paid, that the rate is negotiable and not set by law, that the seller may or may not cover it, and what happens if the seller covers less than the agreed amount. Then let them decide. Don't soften it into vagueness — vagueness is what the rule was written to stop.

"My fee is X. That's negotiable — it isn't set by law and nobody sets it for me. On most listings the seller offers to cover some or all of it, and we'll confirm that on each house before we write. If a seller covers less than X, the difference falls to you, and I'll tell you that number before you make an offer, not after."

Buyers don't resent the fee. They resent finding out about it late. Say it in the first meeting, in one paragraph, and you'll almost never have to say it again.

ND + MN

Full pre-approval before the first showing

Not a pre-qualification, not a rate quote — a lender who has looked at income, credit, and assets. We ask for it before we schedule, and we ask what the buyer is comfortable spending monthly, which is often a different number than what they're approved for.

Showing houses to an unqualified buyer isn't kindness, it's a slow way of hurting them. The worst version is the buyer who falls in love at $340 and finds out in week three they're a $290 buyer. Now nothing at $290 will ever feel like enough.

ND + MN

Showing discipline

Six or seven houses per outing is the ceiling; beyond that nobody remembers anything and everything blurs. Debrief after each one with the same two questions — what worked, what didn't — and write it down. Never leave a buyer alone in a seller's house, never open anything you weren't given access to, and log every showing.

  • A buyer who wants to see twenty houses in a weekend is usually not ready to buy one
  • Repeated "we just want to look at a few more" after a strong fit — there's an unspoken objection, find it
ND + MN

Buyers who won't sign an agreement

Don't argue and don't tour. Explain that it's how the industry works now, that it protects them by putting your fee in writing instead of leaving it ambiguous, and offer a short-term or single-property agreement so they aren't committing to a long relationship with someone they just met.

"I understand not wanting to sign with someone you met twenty minutes ago. Let's do this for one property, or for thirty days. If I'm any good you'll extend it, and if I'm not you shouldn't have to."

A short agreement closes almost every one of these. The buyer isn't objecting to representation, they're objecting to being locked in. Give them an exit and the objection evaporates.

Where deals are made
ND + MN

Call the listing agent before you write

Five minutes on the phone tells you what the seller actually needs: timeline, whether they've got a home to buy, what fell through last time, whether there are other offers, whether they'd rather have price or certainty. Then write to that.

Most agents send offers into a void and then wonder why they lose to a lower number. The winning offer usually isn't the highest — it's the one shaped like what the seller said they needed, from an agent whose name the listing agent already knows.

ND + MN

Multiple offers: run it the same way every time

Tell every agent the same thing about the process, set one deadline, put the seller's instructions in writing, and present every offer received. Do not disclose the terms of one offer to another party unless the seller has instructed you in writing to do so — and think hard before recommending it.

Consistency is the whole defense here. The complaint is never "you ran a multiple offer"; it's "you told them what my number was" or "you gave your own buyer an extra hour." Same information, same deadline, same channel, documented.

  • Seller wanting to shop a written offer around by phone
  • An in-house second offer — escalate to the broker before you do anything else
ND + MN

Appraisal gaps and escalation clauses

An appraisal gap commitment says the buyer will cover up to a stated dollar amount if the appraisal comes in low. Before writing one, confirm the buyer actually has that cash outside their down payment — not on paper, in an account. Never write a gap your buyer can't cover the week the appraisal lands. "We'll figure it out" is how earnest money gets forfeited. And a capped gap ("up to $10,000") is almost always better than an uncapped waiver — it gives the seller the confidence they're buying without handing over an unlimited obligation.

ND + MN

Build the deadline calendar the day you go under contract

Earnest money deposit, inspection period and response, financing application, appraisal ordered, title evidence delivered and objections, well and septic paperwork on Minnesota properties, final walkthrough, closing. Every date on a calendar with a reminder two days ahead, and share it with the client so they can see it too.

ND + MN

Handling the inspection response

Sort every finding into three buckets: safety and structure, deferred maintenance, and cosmetic. Ask for the first bucket. Consider the second. Never ask for the third. A twelve-item list of nail pops tells the seller their buyer is unserious and hands them a reason to look at the backup offer.

Ask for a credit rather than repairs whenever you can. Sellers who are already leaving hire the cheapest contractor available, and now you own the quality of that work as a complaint. Money at closing lets your buyer choose who does it.

Lease vs. purchase agreement
ND + MN

The lease outlives the sale — the buyer is buying a landlord's position

A sale does not terminate a lease. The buyer takes the property subject to the existing tenancy and steps into the seller's shoes: same rent, same term, same tenant rights, same obligations. Nothing about closing gives a new owner the ability to hand a tenant a shorter deadline than the lease already provides.

Say this out loud to the buyer before they write, in exactly these words: you are buying the lease, not just the building. The failure case is an owner-occupant buyer who assumed "the tenants will be out by closing" because nobody ever said otherwise, and now has a mortgage payment, a lease with eight months left, and a family living in a house they can't move into.

A "sale clause" allowing early termination only helps if it was in the lease from the start. You cannot add one at closing, and neither party to the sale can amend a lease the tenant isn't party to.
  • Any listing marketed to owner-occupants with a lease running past the likely closing date
  • A seller who says the tenant "will probably just leave"
  • A verbal or handshake tenancy nobody can produce terms for
MN

Showings in an occupied Minnesota unit: 24 hours, 8 to 8, and real penalties

A landlord may enter only for a reasonable business purpose and after a good-faith effort to give reasonable notice of not less than 24 hours. The notice must specify a time or anticipated window, and entry is limited to 8:00 a.m. to 8:00 p.m. unless the landlord and tenant agree otherwise. Showing the unit to a prospective buyer is expressly a reasonable business purpose — so showings are allowed, on those terms.

A tenant may not waive the right to prior notice, and a landlord may not require waiver as a condition of the lease. Violations entitle the tenant to a penalty that can include a rent reduction up to full rescission of the lease, return of the damage deposit, a civil penalty of up to $500 per violation, and reasonable attorney fees.
Minn. Stat. § 504B.211

"Up to $500 per violation" is per showing. An agent running a busy weekend on a four-plex with sloppy notice can generate a five-figure problem in two days. Build the notice into the showing workflow itself rather than trusting anyone to remember — and a tenant who consents to shorter notice can do so, but get that in writing each time rather than treating it as a standing arrangement.

  • A lockbox on an occupied unit with agents booking directly
  • Showing windows that run past 8:00 p.m. in winter when it's dark by five
  • A landlord who insists the lease already waived notice — it can't
ND

North Dakota entry and deposits on an occupied sale

North Dakota's landlord-tenant chapter governs how notice is given — personal service, conspicuous posting for a reasonable period, or any method resulting in actual notice. On deposits, a lessor requiring a security deposit must hold it in a federally insured interest-bearing account for the tenant's benefit, and the deposit plus accrued interest is paid to the tenant at lease termination subject to lawful deductions.

The consequential part for a sale: the new owner is bound by the deposit obligations even if the deposit was never actually handed over. A buyer who takes the property without collecting the deposits has taken on the liability anyway.
NDCC 47-16-07.1 NDCC 47-16-07.3 NDCC ch. 47-16

Check the lease for its own entry-notice terms and follow whichever is stricter. ND's statutory floor is less prescriptive than Minnesota's, which tempts people into casual showings — and a lease that promises 24 hours is still a contract you can breach.

MNND

Security deposits at closing: Minnesota's 60-day and 20-day mechanics

When the landlord's interest terminates by sale, the successor gets all the rights and obligations as to the deposit. Within 60 days of the termination — or by the time the successor must account to the tenant, whichever comes first — the landlord or their agent must either transfer the deposit to the successor and notify the tenant, or return it to the tenant. Doing one of those relieves the seller of further liability.

The tenant then has 20 days after written notice of the amount transferred to object. If the tenant doesn't object, the successor's obligation to return the deposit is limited to the amount stated in that notice — which is exactly why the written notice matters to the buyer, not just the seller.
Minn. Stat. § 504B.178

That 20-day notice is a genuine protection for your buyer and almost nobody sends it. It converts "whatever the tenant later claims they paid" into a fixed, documented number. Make it part of the post-closing routine, not an afterthought — and note the seller can be relieved of liability while the buyer's exposure is only capped if the notice actually went out.

ND + MN

Where the lease and the purchase agreement collide

These two documents were written by different people at different times for different purposes, and nobody reconciles them until something breaks. Read them side by side before the offer goes in and resolve every conflict in writing.

The estoppel is the one to fight for. Sellers routinely forget a rent concession they gave two years ago, a pet that was allowed verbally, or a deposit that was partly applied. A signed estoppel from each tenant surfaces all of it while the seller still has a reason to be helpful.

ND + MN

The tenant decides how hard this sale is

A tenant with no stake in your outcome controls access to the product you're selling. They can be unreachable, they can decline shorter notice, they can leave dishes out for every showing, and every bit of it is within their rights. Treat them as a party to the transaction, because functionally they are.

"I'm Derek with TruHome and I'm helping your landlord sell the building. First thing: your lease doesn't change because of a sale — a new owner steps into the same lease, same rent, same term. Second: I need to show the unit sometimes, and I'll give you at least 24 hours and a window every time. Tell me what times don't work for you and I'll work around them. If it makes it easier, I'll do the showings in blocks so you're not dealing with this six separate times."

Tell the tenant before the sign goes up, not after they see it. And consider paying for it — a gift card, a cleaning before photos, or a rent credit negotiated with the seller costs almost nothing against the price of a property that shows badly for four months. Hostile tenants have killed more investor sales than bad furnaces.

  • A seller who wants to sell "quietly" without telling the tenant — that never survives the first showing
  • Any suggestion of pressuring a tenant to leave early; that's the seller's lawyer's problem, not your negotiation
ND + MN

Pick the buyer pool before you price it

An occupied rental sells into one of two markets and they value it differently. Investors buy the income — they want the rent roll, the expenses, the deposits, and a tenant in place is an asset. Owner-occupants buy the house and a lease in place is a defect. You cannot market to both well, and trying is how a listing sits.

Decide at the listing appointment, and let the lease term decide for you. Long lease remaining, or multi-unit: price it on income and market it to investors. Short lease or month-to-month with a cooperative tenant: it can be an owner-occupant listing, and the seller's decision about whether to give notice before listing becomes the most consequential one of the whole engagement — which is a conversation for them and their attorney, not advice from you.

  • A single-family rental listed at owner-occupant pricing with 10 months left on a lease
  • Financing: an investor buyer's terms differ from an owner-occupant's, and so does the appraisal approach

Special assessments: certified is payable, pending is a rumor with a number on it

Specials pay for local improvements — paving, sewer, sidewalk, storm — charged against the properties that benefit. The certified number is easy and everybody finds it. The pending number is the one that ends deals, and in Fargo the only way to get it is to ask for a pending search — it is not on the parcel site and it is not on the tax statement. Do it on every listing at intake, not when an offer shows up.

Who pays the specials — and should anyone pay them off?

There's no statutory default. It's allocated by the purchase agreement, and local custom is that the seller pays the current-year installment through closing while the buyer assumes the balance going forward — but custom is not a contract term, so write it down. Flood zone, elevation certificate, and the insurance number

Contract for deed: Minnesota's cancellation clock

A Minnesota contract for deed is cancelled by a statutory procedure, not by a lawsuit and not by the seller changing the locks. The seller serves a notice specifying the default, served like a summons, and the contract terminates after the statutory period unless the purchaser reinstates. The baseline for post–August 1985 contracts is 60 days; contracts executed by an investor seller run 90 days; service by publication runs 90 days from first publication; purchase agreements can be terminated on 30 days.

The purchaser reinstates by curing the default, making payments then due, paying costs of service, paying two percent of the amount in default (excluding a final balloon, taxes, assessments, and assumed mortgages), plus statutory attorney fees. The investor-seller 90-day track additionally requires that the default existed at least 30 days before service and that the seller notified the purchaser of it by certified mail.
Minn. Stat. § 559.21

We do not run cancellations and we do not draft contracts for deed. What we do is recognize one, tell the client the clock is statutory and unforgiving, and get them to a real estate attorney the same week. A seller who "just wants them out" and starts self-helping creates liability that dwarfs the arrearage.

MN

MN ch. 559A: the investor-seller CD rules

Effective for contracts executed on or after August 1, 2024, Minnesota added a disclosure regime for residential contracts for deed with an investor seller — and the definition is broad, treating a residential CD seller as an investor seller unless they fit one of the statutory exceptions. The headline requirement is a waiting period: the investor seller may not enter into the contract for deed until ten calendar days after the purchase agreement is executed and the required disclosures and cancellation instructions are delivered.

The disclosures are on top of the ordinary §§ 513.52–513.61 seller disclosures, must be affixed to the front of the purchase agreement (or delivered as a separate document at least ten days out if there's no purchase agreement), and come with instructions for cancellation. The chapter also creates a rebuttable presumption against "churning" — repeat terminations on the same property or across a seller's portfolio.
Minn. Stat. ch. 559A

If a client asks you to help them sell on contract for deed to a buyer who can't qualify, the first question is whether they're an investor seller under this chapter — and the honest answer is usually "probably, and this now has a ten-day clock, a disclosure package, and real exposure." That's a conversation to have with counsel before, not after.

ND + MN

Manufactured homes: is it real property or a titled vehicle?

Everything downstream depends on this answer — financing, conveyance, taxation, and whether you're even doing a real estate transaction. A manufactured home is real property only when it's permanently affixed to a foundation on land the homeowner owns and the title has been surrendered through the statutory affixation process. Otherwise it's titled personal property, and a home on rented park land generally cannot be converted at all.

MN uses a title/MCO surrender process for a home affixed to owner-occupied land. ND ties surrender to a recorded affidavit of affixation, with the requirements deemed satisfied as of the recording date if the application reaches the department within sixty days of recording; the home is then conveyed and encumbered under ch. 47-10 like other real property. The trap: North Dakota's department may not accept a certificate of title for surrender until all security interests and liens on it have been released. An existing chattel lender has to be dealt with first, and that takes time nobody budgets for.
Minn. Stat. § 168A.141 NDCC 39-05-35 Minn. Stat. ch. 327C
  • Listing a titled manufactured home on the MLS as though it were real property
  • A buyer with conventional financing on a home that was never affixed
MN

New construction: Minnesota's statutory warranties run with the house

Minnesota imposes warranties by statute on the sale of a new dwelling: one year against defects from faulty workmanship and defective materials due to noncompliance with building standards, two years against defects from faulty installation of plumbing, electrical, heating and cooling due to noncompliance, and ten years against major construction defects due to noncompliance.

These warranties survive the passing of title — so a resale buyer of a four-year-old house may still be inside the ten-year major-defect window. They also aren't wiped out by the dissolution of a builder that was a corporation or LLC.
Minn. Stat. § 327A.02

This is a selling point almost nobody uses. On a newer Minnesota resale with a structural question, the remaining statutory warranty is real information for a nervous buyer — and on the listing side, a seller sitting on an unresolved builder issue needs to know the clock exists before they decide to say nothing.

MNND

Mechanic's liens: the recently remodeled house

A house that had work done in the last few months carries lien exposure that hasn't surfaced yet, because a lien can be recorded well after the contractor left. In Minnesota, subcontractors and materialmen generally must serve a pre-lien notice within 45 days of first furnishing, and a lien statement must be recorded within 120 days of last furnishing. That 120-day tail is the window your closing can fall into.

North Dakota runs on different numbers. A construction lien is recorded with the county recorder within 90 days of last performance, and written notice that a lien will be claimed must go to the legal or equitable owner by certified mail at least ten days before recording. If the owner demands a statement of account, that adds another ten days — which can eat the recording window if the claimant started late. For validity against the owner, the filing runs within three years of first furnishing.
Minn. Stat. § 514.011 NDCC ch. 35-27 NDCC 35-27-02

Don't carry Minnesota's numbers across the river. MN runs a 45-day pre-lien notice and a 120-day recording window from last furnishing; ND runs a 10-day certified-mail notice and 90 days. The ND certified-mail notice is also the useful early warning — if your seller received one, there is an unpaid contractor and you want the closer involved today.

ND + MN

Foreclosure and redemption: the periods aren't close

If you're working REO, short sales, or a distressed seller, the redemption period governs when anyone can actually deliver clear title — and the two states are far apart.

Minnesota: the mortgagor may redeem within six months after the foreclosure sale under § 580.23, with a five-week period available for certain abandoned properties under § 582.032. North Dakota: redemption within sixty days after the sale under § 32-19-18, except abandoned property — and agricultural land carries a much longer period running from the filing of the summons and complaint or first publication.
Minn. Stat. § 580.23 Minn. Stat. § 582.032 NDCC 32-19-18

Never quote a closing date on a property still in a redemption period without the closer confirming it. And a distressed seller inside redemption has options with deadlines attached — that's a conversation for counsel and a HUD-approved counselor, and the referral is the service.

Severed minerals: the buyer probably isn't getting them

Across much of North Dakota the mineral estate was severed from the surface generations ago, and a great many surface owners have no minerals at all. The abstract and the attorney's title opinion will show it — which is one more reason not to treat title work as a formality. Buyers of acreage very often assume minerals come along, and nobody has ever corrected them.

ND mineral abandonment: use it or lose it

North Dakota treats a mineral interest as abandoned if it goes unused for twenty years preceding the first publication of the statutory notice, unless the owner records a statement of claim. The record owner can preserve the interest by filing a statement of claim within sixty days of first publication. On abandonment, title to the mineral interest vests in the surface owner.

"Used" is defined broadly by statute — production, drilling, and certain injection, withdrawal, storage, or disposal operations can all count. This is genuinely technical and litigated; the citation is here so you recognize the issue, not so you resolve it.
NDCC ch. 38-18.1 NDCC 38-18.1-02

If a client tells you they inherited minerals somewhere in North Dakota and have never done anything with them, the useful thing you can say is: talk to a mineral attorney about whether anything needs to be recorded. That single sentence has been worth a great deal of money to people.

ND + MN

Corporate farming: the entity buyer who legally can't buy

Both states restrict corporate and entity ownership of farmland. North Dakota broadly prohibits corporations and LLCs from owning or leasing land used for farming or ranching and from engaging in the business of farming, subject to exceptions — notably a family-farm structure where the officers, directors, or managers are shareholders or members actively engaged in farming, with one operating or residing on the operation. Minnesota similarly bars corporations, LLCs, pension and investment funds, irrevocable trusts, and limited partnerships from farming or acquiring an interest in agricultural land unless an exception applies.

This is the trap: a buyer says "put it in my LLC for liability," everyone nods, and nobody checks. Ask the ownership-structure question at the offer stage on any ag parcel, and route it to counsel before the entity is named as buyer. Restructuring a week before closing is expensive; discovering the purchase was prohibited afterward is much worse.

NDCC ch. 10-06.1 Minn. Stat. § 500.24

  • A 1031 exchange landing in an entity on ag ground

MN

Green Acres and Rural Preserve: the tax bill the sale triggers

These Minnesota programs defer a portion of property tax on qualifying agricultural land. When land comes out of the program, the deferred tax gets paid back — the current year plus the two prior years — along with any deferred special assessments plus interest once the whole parcel is removed.

Find out whether a parcel is enrolled before you price it and before your seller nets out their proceeds. A payback that shows up on the settlement statement, unmentioned until closing, is the kind of surprise sellers remember permanently. County assessors are helpful on this and it's a quick call.

Minn. Stat. § 273.111 MN Revenue — Green Acres
ND + MN

Cropland runs on a different calendar

Possession of tillable ground is negotiated around the crop year, not around a moving truck. Before you write anything on ag land, establish: who has the ground this year and under what lease, whether the lease is written or a handshake, when it terminates, who gets the growing crop, whether there's CRP or other program enrollment that follows the land, and who holds the tile, fencing, and access rights.

"Access by a neighbor's goodwill" deserves its own line, because it is astonishingly common and it evaporates the day the property changes hands. If access isn't of record, say so in writing before someone buys a landlocked parcel from a handshake.

ND + MN

Tribal trust land: recognize it and stop

Both states contain substantial land held in trust by the federal government for tribes or individual tribal members. Trust land is not conveyed like fee land — it involves federal approval, and jurisdiction, leasing, financing, and taxation all work differently. Reservation boundaries also contain a checkerboard of fee, trust, and allotted parcels, so being "on the reservation" doesn't settle the question either way.

This is a stop-and-ask, not a research project. If a parcel might be trust or allotted land, the answer is the tribe's realty office, the BIA, and counsel experienced in Indian land — before you take the listing, not after you have a buyer. Guessing here isn't a risk you can manage with a disclosure.

MNND

Transfer paperwork: two states, two entirely different gates

Minnesota charges a state deed tax and requires an electronic Certificate of Real Estate Value. North Dakota has no comparable transfer tax but conditions recording on a certified statement of full consideration written on the face of the deed.

Minnesota: an eCRV must be filed when property is sold or conveyed for consideration greater than $3,000 (consideration includes debt assumed); at or below that, no CRV. Minn. Stat. § 287.21 MN eCRV NDCC 11-18-02.2

You are not preparing these — the closer is. MN well disclosure: it gates the recording too

Before signing an agreement to sell or transfer, a Minnesota seller must disclose in writing the number, status, and location of all known wells, with a sketch map, marking each as in use, not in use, or sealed. The requirement reaches contract-for-deed sales as well. And it has teeth at the county: a recorder or registrar may not record a conveyance requiring a certificate of value unless the instrument carries the required statement or is accompanied by a well disclosure certificate.

ND + MN

Disputed earnest money: you are a stakeholder, not a judge

When a deal dies and both sides demand the money, the broker holding it is caught between two claimants. You do not decide who's right, and you do not release on your own read of the contract — releasing to the wrong party makes the brokerage liable to the other one for the full amount.

The instinct to "just be fair" is exactly the instinct that creates liability. Being scrupulously neutral and slightly unhelpful is the correct posture here, and both parties will respect it more than they'll admit at the time.

ND + MN

RESPA Section 8: what you can't take, and what an affiliate doesn't fix

Federal law prohibits giving or accepting anything of value for the referral of settlement service business on a federally related mortgage loan, and prohibits splitting fees except for services actually performed. That reaches title companies, lenders, closers, home warranty companies, and inspectors — everyone whose business you're in a position to send.

An affiliated business arrangement is an exception only if its conditions are met — and it is not a wrapper that legitimizes referral payments: paying a dividend or anything else of value to an affiliate based on the amount of business referred falls outside the exemption and violates Section 8. A franchise agreement can't be used to insulate against kickbacks either. Ordinary promotional and educational activity is permitted, and you can compensate your own employees, but not affiliates, for referrals.
12 CFR § 1024.15 CFPB RESPA FAQs

The practical test: am I being paid, in money or in kind, in a way that tracks how much business I send? If yes, stop, regardless of what the agreement is titled. Marketing services agreements are the usual vehicle — they're lawful only when real, market-rate services are actually delivered and the payment doesn't move with referral volume. Anything that looks like a desk fee for leads, subsidized marketing, or a title company covering your event goes to the broker first.

  • A lender or title company offering to "co-market" in a way that only benefits you
  • Any arrangement whose value scales with closings sent
  • Free or below-cost services from a settlement provider

Minnesota renewal: June 30, and it is the primary broker's job

Minnesota real estate licenses run 24 months and expire June 30 of the assigned expiration year. The renewal window opens in early April and closes June 30. And the part people miss: Commerce puts the duty on the brokerage — it is the primary broker's responsibility to renew all licenses due for renewal and associated with the company, done as a mass renewal through the licensing portal.

Renew at pulseportal.com. Portal support 833-814-0228. Licensing questions: licensing.commerce@state.mn.us, 651-539-1599 or 877-876-4430. Nonresident salespersons must get a Letter of Certification from their resident state or CE certificates in before the deadline or the license lapses.
MN Commerce — renewals Minn. Stat. § 82.63 subd. 5

Run a roster audit every April 1, not June. Pull every licensee attached to the company, check each one's expiration date and CE transcript in the portal, and chase the gaps while there is still time to take a class. June 30 is the day you find out, and by then there is nothing left to do about it.

  • Agents who joined mid-cycle — their expiration date is not the same as everyone else's
  • Anyone who transferred in during the license period
  • Nonresident licensees whose certification letter has to come from another state on someone else's timeline
MN

The Minnesota CE math, and why it fails quietly

30 hours across the 24-month cycle, and — this is the trap — at least 15 of those hours must be completed before the previous June 30. So the cycle has a midpoint deadline that nobody gets a reminder about. The 30 must include 1 hour of fair housing, 1 hour of agency law, and the required module course.

Extra hours do not carry over into the next period. No CE is accepted after June 30. There is no partial credit and no grace period for coursework.
MN Commerce — education

The most common way a good agent loses a license is the year-one 15 hours. They take 30 hours in year two, thinking the total is what matters, and discover in June that the first-year requirement was missed twelve months ago and cannot be repaired. Check both halves of the cycle at your April audit, not just the total.

MN

Transferring brokerages: the trap that eats a license

When a salesperson terminates activity on behalf of a broker, the salesperson's license becomes ineffective immediately — not at the end of the month, not when the new brokerage gets around to it. The former broker must notify the commissioner within ten days of the termination on the prescribed form. The salesperson may apply to transfer to active status with a new broker at any time during the remainder of the license period.

Here is the sharp edge: if the transfer is not requested before the license period ends, the commissioner requires an application for a new license — not a renewal, and not a reinstatement. A mid-cycle move that never got transferred before June 30 doesn't leave you with a lapsed license to revive. It leaves you starting over.
Minn. Stat. § 82.63 subd. 6

This is the single most likely explanation when a new agent's Minnesota license has evaporated after a brokerage change. It usually isn't a CE failure and it usually isn't the state losing paperwork — it's a transfer that sat in someone's inbox across a June 30. Treat an incoming agent's transfer as a same-week task with a written confirmation back, every time.

  • An incoming agent who "thinks" the old brokerage submitted the termination
  • A transfer submitted but never confirmed — submission is not completion
  • Anyone joining in May or June, when the transfer window and the renewal window overlap
MN

Reinstatement: the two-year outer wall

"Two years" gets repeated as though it were one rule. It is actually several, and which one you're under changes the fix.

Failure to renew for two years — the commissioner requires the licensee to take the examination again. A lapse for failure to file a timely renewal — the commissioner may institute a revocation or suspension proceeding within two years after the license was last effective, and enter that order as of the last date the license was in effect. A license cancelled for failure to complete post-licensing education — reinstated without re-examination by completing the instruction, applying, and paying the fee within two years of cancellation. And renewal of a salesperson's license is not effective beyond two years after it was granted absent evidence of compliance with the education requirement.
Minn. Stat. § 82.62 Minn. Stat. § 82.63 subd. 6

So there are at least four different two-year rules and one no-reinstatement-at-all path — the unfinished brokerage transfer under § 82.63, which produces a brand-new application rather than any kind of revival. Establish which one applies before you spend a dollar or a week, because the forms, fees, and timelines all differ. That single question, asked first, is the whole shortcut.

Whichever path applies, the licensee cannot conduct real estate business in Minnesota in the meantime. Not "quietly," not "just finishing up a file," not under someone else's supervision.

Fixing a lapsed license the right way

There is no clever route through this and anyone offering one is selling something. What there is: a correct order of operations that gets it resolved faster and leaves a record if it goes sideways.

Two things make these calls go better. First, ask how the rule works, not what should I do about my situation — regulators answer the first freely and can't answer the second. Second, put a dated timeline in front of them. What a lapsed agent can still do — and what they absolutely cannot

A licensee whose Minnesota license is lapsed is, in Minnesota, an unlicensed person. But a license is state-specific, so an agent licensed in both states who loses one keeps the other. That's the legitimate shortcut worth knowing: redeploy them across the river while the problem is fixed.

Can still do, in the lapsed state: unlicensed administrative work — scheduling, coordinating, data entry, marketing production. Cannot do: show property, discuss terms, negotiate, hold themselves out as an agent, or be paid per transaction. Can still do fully: licensed work in a state where their license is active.

Say the boundary out loud to the agent and put it in an email, on the day. This protects them more than it protects you — a new agent who doesn't know where the line is will cross it trying to be helpful, and it is genuinely hard to explain later that nobody told her.

  • "Just shadowing" at a showing — that's showing property
  • Answering a buyer's question about terms on a text thread
  • Her name on marketing for a listing in the lapsed state

Compensation earned while unlicensed is not collectable

Minnesota bars anyone from bringing or maintaining an action to collect compensation for acts requiring a license without alleging and proving they were duly licensed at the time the cause of action arose. Being licensed again by the time you sue does not repair a commission earned during a lapse.

The statute also requires a written agreement before a person required to be licensed can maintain an action for a commission or fee on a purchase, sale, lease, or conveyance.
Minn. Stat. § 82.85

This is the reason "let's just get this one to closing quietly" is never worth it. The commission on that file may be uncollectable anyway, the brokerage carries the exposure for unlicensed activity, and you'd have traded a real license for a fee you can't sue for. Reassign the file and pay the agent a bonus later if you want to make them whole — that's a compensation decision, and a cleaner one.

Wholesaling: marketing an equitable interest requires a license in ND

North Dakota's exemption statute carries an express limitation that answers a question people ask constantly: marketing equitable interests in purchase contracts between owners and buyers requires licensing, regardless of any other exemption in the section. So "I'm not selling the house, I'm assigning my contract" does not sidestep the license requirement in North Dakota. NDCC 43-23-07

Two live situations. When an investor asks us to help them wholesale, the answer is that the marketing side is licensed activity here and we're not building a workaround.   • "Assignment fee" appearing on a settlement statement nobody expected
  • A seller who doesn't know their contract is being marketed to other buyers
  • A purchase agreement naming "and/or assigns" on a deal you're bringing to a seller client

North Dakota renewal: a different calendar entirely

North Dakota runs annually, not biennially, and the dates don't line up with Minnesota's at any point. ND REC — education ND REC — 701-328-9749

A Minnesota license going down for weeks over a clerical problem rather than a substantive one is a familiar regional pattern — common enough that it does not surprise the people who administer these rules for a living. The mistake is designing the brokerage as though a license outage is a freak event. It isn't. Somewhere in the next few cycles, someone here will lose a Minnesota license for about a month over paperwork, and the only variables you control are whether you find out early, whether you can prove what you submitted, and whether the business keeps running while it's fixed. Fair housing

No exceptions
ND + MN

There are three lists, and the federal one is the shortest

Almost every fair housing mistake at a brokerage starts with someone working from the seven federal classes they learned in licensing school. In this market that list is the floor, and it is not the one that governs. The most protective list that applies to the property is the one you follow.

Federal (FHA): race, color, religion, sex, national origin, familial status, disability. Minnesota (§ 363A.09): race, color, creed, religion, national origin, sex, gender identity, marital status, status with regard to public assistance, disability, sexual orientation, familial status. North Dakota (ch. 14-02.5 / § 14-02.4-12): race, color, religion, sex, national origin, age, disability, familial status, marital status, and status with respect to public assistance. City of Fargo adds more still, including sexual orientation and participation in lawful activity off the premises during nonworking hours.
Minn. Stat. § 363A.09 NDCC ch. 14-02.5 NDCC 14-02.4-12 Fargo Human Relations Ordinance

Three differences will actually catch someone here. Age is protected in North Dakota and is not a federal class — "perfect for young professionals" is a violation in Fargo in a way it isn't federally. Marital status and creed are protected in both states. And sexual orientation and gender identity are expressly protected statewide in Minnesota, are not in North Dakota's state statute, but are covered by Fargo's city ordinance — so in North Dakota the answer genuinely depends on which city the property sits in. Federal interpretation on this has moved more than once; don't build a practice on it when the state and local text says what it says.

  • An agent applying "the seven federal classes" to a Minnesota listing
  • An ND property outside Fargo where someone assumes the city ordinance applies
  • Any policy written once and never re-checked against the city it operates in
ND + MN

Describe the property, never the people

"Walkable to three elementary schools" is a fact about the property. "Great for families" is a statement about who should live there. The test is simple: does the sentence describe the dwelling, or does it describe who should live in it? Rewrite around the layout and the features and let people decide for themselves whom it suits.

Recurring offenders in listing copy and rental ads: adults only, adult community, mature, no children, perfect for couples, ideal for young professionals, great for a young family, quiet adult building, perfect for the physically fit, walking distance (a mobility issue), master bedroom where a house style is meant, and anything naming a church, congregation, or ethnic community as a selling point. Familial status is the single most-violated class in advertising.
HUD FHEO

Run every listing description through one question before it goes live: if I deleted every word about people, would the ad still sell the house? It always would. The demographic adjectives were never doing work — they just felt warm to write.

  • AI-generated listing copy, which loves "perfect for" constructions — read every word before publishing
  • Photos chosen to signal who lives in a building
  • Social posts, which are advertising and are held to the same standard
ND + MN

Steering, and the questions you must never answer

Steering is directing someone toward or away from an area based on a protected class — and it is usually done kindly, by an agent who thinks they're being helpful. It does not require intent, and "the client asked me to" is not a defense; the client's preference does not become your permission.

"I can't characterize neighborhoods by who lives there — that's fair housing law, and honestly you'd get a better answer from the data than from me. Here's the school district information, the city's crime statistics, and census data for the area. I'd also just spend an evening there. If you tell me the features you want — schools, commute, lot size, price — I'll show you everything that matches."

The trap questions are "is it a safe neighborhood," "how are the schools," and "what kind of people live there." All three invite you to characterize residents. Answer all three the same way: point to published data, never summarize it in your own words, and never let the set of properties you show narrow based on an assumption nobody asked you to make.

  • A seller instructing you about who they will or won't sell to — that ends the listing
  • Quietly not sending listings in certain areas because you assumed a preference
  • "You'd probably be more comfortable over in ___" — the classic steering sentence
MNND

Public assistance status and housing vouchers

Both states protect status with regard to public assistance, and Fargo's ordinance does too. Whether that specifically forces a landlord to accept a Section 8 Housing Choice Voucher is a narrower and genuinely contested question — it has been litigated in Minnesota, and Minneapolis and St. Paul passed express ordinances requiring acceptance, which survived a long constitutional challenge in Fletcher Properties v. City of Minneapolis.

Those city ordinances do not reach Moorhead, Crookston, or Fargo. But the underlying public assistance protection in both state statutes does apply here — and the boundary between "we don't participate in that program" and "we don't rent to people on assistance" is exactly where liability lives.
Fletcher Properties v. Minneapolis Edwards v. Hopkins Plaza Minn. Stat. § 363A.09

Do not let any TruHome-managed property run an advertised blanket "no Section 8" policy, and do not let an owner instruct you into one verbally. This is an area where the law differs by city, has moved in court, and is actively legislated — which means a policy written three years ago is not evidence that it's still fine. If an owner wants a voucher policy, that goes to counsel before it goes in an ad.

  • "No Section 8" in any listing, ad, or voicemail greeting
  • A minimum-income rule applied to the tenant's full rent when a voucher covers most of it
  • An owner's verbal instruction you carried out without writing down
ND + MN

Assistance animals: HUD moved in 2026, and that is not permission

On May 22, 2026 HUD permanently rescinded its 2020 assistance-animal notice (FHEO-2020-01) and adopted a narrower enforcement posture: FHEO will find reasonable cause and recommend charges only where the animal has been individually trained to provide assistance related to the person's disability — aligning HUD's enforcement with the ADA's service-animal definition.

Read that precisely. It changed what HUD will prosecute. It did not amend the Fair Housing Act, and it has no effect on the Minnesota Human Rights Act or North Dakota's Housing Discrimination Act, which are separately enforced by the MDHR and the ND Department of Labor and Human Rights. A private lawsuit under the FHA also does not depend on HUD's charging posture.
Analysis of the rescission Minn. Stat. § 363A.09 ND Housing Discrimination Act

Anyone who reads a headline about this and adopts a blanket "no emotional support animals" policy is making a decision on the strength of one enforcement agency's posture while two state agencies and the private bar are unaffected. Our position stays where it was: take every request, run the accommodation analysis, document it, decide case by case. If we ever change that, it will be on advice of counsel and in writing — not because the federal posture shifted.

  • Charging a pet deposit, pet rent, or pet fee for an assistance animal
  • Asking for a diagnosis rather than for verification of a disability-related need
  • Applying a breed, size, or weight restriction to an assistance animal without an individualized assessment
  • Any staff member citing "HUD changed the rules" as a reason to deny
ND + MN

Handling an accommodation or modification request

Two different things with two different cost rules. A reasonable accommodation is a change to a rule, policy, practice, or service — a parking space near the door, a rent due date moved to match a benefits deposit, an assistance animal in a no-pets building. A reasonable modification is a physical change to the unit, like a ramp or grab bars; in private housing the tenant generally bears that cost.

Denials are rarely what sink a brokerage here — silence is. A request that sat for six weeks while everyone waited for someone else to handle it looks exactly like refusal, and it's indefensible in a way that a documented, reasoned decision never is.

ND + MN

Criminal history screening: the 2025 rescission is a trap

In November 2025 HUD rescinded the guidance that had governed criminal-history screening for a decade — Notice PIH 2015-19, the 2016 Office of General Counsel guidance, and the 2022 FHEO memo — and in January 2026 proposed removing its disparate-impact regulation entirely.

None of that repealed the Fair Housing Act, and the disparate-impact framework from Texas Dept. of Housing & Community Affairs v. Inclusive Communities Project remains binding law that courts continue to apply. State human rights acts are untouched. A blanket criminal-history ban still carries real fair housing exposure — the agency that used to warn you about it simply stopped saying so.
Inclusive Communities (2015) Analysis of the rescissions

Keep doing what the withdrawn guidance described, because it was always just good practice: no denials on arrest records alone, individualized assessment rather than a bright-line lookback, consideration of the nature of the offense, how long ago, and evidence of rehabilitation — and the same standard applied to every applicant. If we ever loosen this, it will be a written decision reviewed by counsel, not an agent's improvisation on a Tuesday.

ND + MN

Written screening criteria are the whole defense

Almost every defensible tenant-selection decision looks the same from the outside: published criteria, applied identically, with the reason for each denial written down. Almost every indefensible one involves a judgment call made in the moment that nobody recorded.

Testers are real and they work in pairs. The defense against a paired test is not that your staff were careful that day — it's that the criteria were published, identical, and documented, so two applicants with the same file provably got the same answer.

ND + MN

Occupancy standards and familial status

Under HUD's long-standing Keating analysis, a two-person-per-bedroom standard is presumptively reasonable — but that presumption is rebuttable, and HUD has pulled back from treating it as a bright line. The analysis also weighs the size of the bedrooms and the unit, the ages of the children, the unit's configuration, physical limits like septic or building systems, and state and local codes.

"Two per bedroom" is a starting point, not a shield — and an occupancy rule that consistently screens out families with children is a familial status problem however neutrally it's worded. On a large unit with big bedrooms, a rigid two-per-bedroom cap is the version most likely to draw a complaint. Tie the number to something real, like the septic capacity or the local code, and write down what it's tied to.

  • An occupancy cap counting infants the same as adults
  • A rule applied to a four-bedroom house that was written for studios
  • "One person per bedroom" — almost never defensible
ND + MN

If a complaint arrives

Complaints go to different agencies with different clocks, and more than one can run at once. North Dakota complaints go to the Department of Labor and Human Rights and must be filed within 300 days of the discriminatory act. Minnesota complaints go to the Department of Human Rights. HUD takes complaints under the FHA, and Fargo's Human Rights Commission takes complaints under the city ordinance.

The day one rules: tell the designated broker immediately; do not contact the complainant; preserve everything — applications, texts, showing logs, screening notes, ad copy as published; write down what happened while it's fresh; and get counsel engaged before any response goes out. Never let the agent involved respond on their own.
ND Dept. of Labor & Human Rights MN Dept. of Human Rights Fargo Human Rights Commission

The instinct to call the complainant and clear it up is the single most damaging thing anyone can do here, and it is always well-intentioned. It creates a witness account of your explanation before you know the facts, and in a retaliation claim it becomes the retaliation. Preserve, escalate, say nothing.

Advertising & marketing

Whose name is on it
ND + MN

The brokerage name goes on everything

Minnesota requires any licensee advertising to clearly and conspicuously display the real estate brokerage name. North Dakota allows a licensee's name and contact information provided the brokerage's registered or trade name and contact information are also clearly included. This covers every medium — yard signs, flyers, websites, and every social post.

Minn. Stat. § 82.69 NDAC 70-02-03-02.1
  • Personal Instagram or Facebook posts about a listing with no brokerage identification
  • Video walkthroughs where the brokerage name never appears
ND + MN

Team names need broker authorization

A team is licensees at the same brokerage, under the designated broker's supervision, working together and holding themselves out under a team name. Minnesota permits the team name in advertising only when the primary broker of the brokerage authorized it. North Dakota's advertising rule applies to team advertising the same way it applies to individual advertising.

The failure mode is a team name that reads like an independent brokerage — bigger than the brokerage name, its own logo, its own domain with no TruHome identification. That's the version that draws a complaint.

Management crossover

The other half of TruHome
ND + MN

Some sale leads are really management leads

An owner calling to sell a rental because they're tired of it is a management conversation before it's a listing conversation. Run both numbers honestly: net proceeds from a sale against net cash flow under management, with real vacancy and maintenance assumptions. Then let them choose.

Selling them the listing when management was the better answer costs you the listing once. Doing the math in front of them and recommending against your own commission is how you get the listing when they actually are ready, plus the referrals in between. This is the single highest-return habit in the business.

ND + MN

Keep the money separate

Sales earnest money and property management operating funds are different pools with different rules, and they never touch. Owner funds, security deposits, and earnest money each stay in their own account per the applicable state's requirements.

A brokerage doing both sales and management has twice the trust-account exposure and exactly one license. Treat every transfer between pools as something that needs a reason written down.

ND + MN

Management work is licensed work

Leasing, listing rentals, procuring tenants, and negotiating leases are brokerage activity in both states. Minnesota's definition of "real estate broker" reaches anyone who, for another and for commission, fee, or other valuable consideration, lists, sells, exchanges, buys, rents, manages, or negotiates real estate transactions — management and rental are inside the definition, not adjacent to it. A salesperson is someone acting on behalf of a broker in performing those acts.

North Dakota exempts a bona fide owner or lessor — and their regular employees — managing their own residential or agricultural holdings. The chapter's other exemptions cover attorneys acting through estate, guardianship, trust, bankruptcy, or receivership proceedings with court approval; auctioneers at advertised public auctions; banks and trust companies acting through their officers; a power-of-attorney holder on a non-recurring transaction not designed to evade licensing; court-appointed receivers, trustees, executors, and guardians; and public officers acting officially.
Minn. Stat. § 82.55 NDCC 43-23-07 NDCC ch. 43-23
Read the ND exemption narrowly: it is the owner's own employees managing the owner's own property. It does not cover an unlicensed person managing property for someone else, however the arrangement is labeled — and a power of attorney is exempt only for a non-recurring transaction, not as a standing workaround.

Where this gets uncomfortable is with capable unlicensed staff who have been doing the work for years. Draw the line by task, in writing: showing units, quoting terms, and negotiating leases are licensed activities; scheduling, coordinating, and administration are not. Getting that boundary in writing protects the person doing the work as much as the brokerage.

Objections & scripts

What to actually say
ND + MN

How to run any objection

Four moves, in order: acknowledge it without flinching, ask a question that finds the real concern underneath it, answer the real one, then ask for the decision. Most agents skip straight to answering, which means they spend their best material on the wrong objection.

Nearly every objection is one of three things wearing a costume: they don't trust the number, they don't trust you, or they're not actually ready to move. "Your commission is too high" is almost never about the commission. Find out which of the three you're dealing with before you open your mouth, or you'll answer a question nobody asked.

ND + MN

"Your commission is too high"

Usually means: I don't see what I'm getting for it, or someone quoted me less.

"That's fair — it's a real number and you should push on it. Can I ask what you're comparing it to? … Here's what I'd say: my fee is negotiable, it isn't set by law and nobody sets it for me. What I'd rather you compare is net proceeds, not rate. The last four listings I took at this fee sold for more than the list price the discount brokers were quoting, and that difference was bigger than the fee gap. If I can't show you that on your house specifically, you shouldn't hire me."

Never defend the fee by calling it standard — it isn't, and saying so is a legal problem, not just a weak argument. Defend it with net proceeds and a specific plan. And be willing to actually lose one over it; the agent who never loses a listing on fee is charging too little.

ND + MN

"Another agent said they could get more"

Usually means: I want the higher number to be true and I'm hoping you'll match it.

"They might be right — did they show you the comparable sales behind it? … Here's the risk with buying a number instead of a plan: any agent can say a number to get a signature, and then you spend ninety days finding out. Ask them for three closed sales in the last six months that support it. If they've got them, take the listing to them and I'll mean that. If they don't, you've learned something important about how they'll handle the rest of your sale."

Never match a number you can't defend to win a listing. You're buying ninety days of a stale listing, two awkward reduction conversations, and a seller who ends up blaming you for a number you didn't believe. Losing that listing is the good outcome.

ND + MN

"We want to try it ourselves first"

Usually means: I don't want to pay a commission if this is easy.

"That's a reasonable thing to try, and some people do fine with it. Two things I'd want you to know before you start. First, the disclosure paperwork is where unrepresented sellers get hurt — the liability runs for two years after closing in Minnesota, and it's real. Second, most buyers coming to you will be represented, so you'll be negotiating against a professional on your own biggest asset. Give it three weeks. If you get it done, genuinely well done. If you're getting tire-kickers and lowballs, call me and I'll tell you honestly whether it's price or exposure."

Don't argue with a FSBO and don't disparage the choice. Set the three-week checkpoint, then actually call on day twenty-two. Half of them list, and they list with the person who was straight with them instead of the six agents who called to say it couldn't be done.

ND + MN

"Zillow says it's worth more"

Usually means: give me a reason to believe your number instead.

"Those models are a starting point and they're not bad at averages — what they can't do is walk through the house. They don't know your kitchen was redone in 2019, they don't know the neighbor's is the same floor plan with a finished basement and it closed at $298, and they can't see the roof. Let me show you the three closed sales I'd use and what I adjusted for. If you think one of my adjustments is wrong, tell me — that's a conversation worth having."

ND + MN

"Why do I have to sign anything just to look at houses?"

Usually means: I don't want to be locked in with someone I just met.

"Because as of August 2024 that's how it works everywhere — I can't tour a house with you without one, and Minnesota required it long before that. But hear what it actually does for you: it puts my fee in writing, in a number, before you see a single house. The old way, that was ambiguous until closing. If the length is what's bothering you, let's do thirty days, or just this one property. If I'm any good you'll extend it."

Lead with what it does for them, not with the rule. "It's required" ends the conversation without winning it; "it puts my fee in writing before you see a house" actually answers the fear.

ND + MN

The price reduction ask

Usually means: you need to make this the market's verdict, not your opinion.

"Here's where we are: 24 showings, no offers, and three sets of feedback saying the same thing. That's the market telling us something, and it isn't about the marketing — we got the traffic. The good news is showings with no offers is a price problem, and price problems are the fixable kind. I want to go to $299,900. Not $302,000 — that changes nothing, because buyers searching under $300 never see us. Are you comfortable with that, or do you want to give it two more weeks first?"

Bring data, propose a specific number, and offer them the choice of timing. Sellers accept reductions they helped decide. And if you signed a reduction schedule at the listing table, this whole conversation is just "we're at day 21" — which is why you sign it at the table.

ND + MN

"Can you cut your fee to make this deal work?"

Usually means: we're $4,000 apart and I'm looking for the easiest place to find it.

"I might. Before I do, let's make sure it's the right lever — if I contribute, I want it to actually close the gap, not just move the negotiation. What's the seller's real bottom line? … Here's what I'll do: I'll come to the table for $X if we can get it signed today at $Y. That's me buying certainty, not me admitting the fee was wrong."

Cutting your fee is a legitimate tool at the very end of a negotiation to close a real gap. It is not a tool for the middle, and it should never be the first concession offered — the moment you volunteer it, it becomes the starting point for every future ask. Make it conditional and make it final.

ND + MN

Delivering a low appraisal

The goal is not to soften it. The goal is to get to options in under ninety seconds.

"The appraisal came in at $292 against our $305 contract. Not what we wanted. Here's where we stand: the lender will lend on $292, so there are four ways this goes — the seller comes down, the buyer brings the difference in cash, we split it, or we challenge the appraisal with better comps. I think the challenge is worth trying because two of the comps used are across the highway. I'd like to do that first and talk again Thursday. Does that work?"

Bad news goes out fast, in writing, with options attached. The instinct is to wait until you have a solution — resist it. Clients forgive bad news; they don't forgive learning that you sat on it for a day.

ND + MN

"We're going to wait until spring"

Usually means: something else isn't ready, and spring is a polite way to end the conversation.

"That's often the right call — can I ask what's driving it? … Worth knowing: inventory is thinnest right now and buyers who are looking in January are looking because they have to move, not because they're browsing. Serious buyers, less competition. Come spring you'll have more buyers and a lot more houses next to yours. If waiting is about getting the house ready, let's use the time — I'll walk through and tell you the three things worth doing and the six that aren't."

Never fight the timing. Find the reason behind it and solve for that instead. In our market, "wait until spring" is very often "we can't afford the repairs right now" or "one of us isn't sure" — and neither of those is solved by a market statistic.

ND + MN

The expired listing call

They've been called eleven times today. Be the one who isn't pitching.

"Mr. Hanson, I'm sure your phone has been busy today, so I'll be quick. I'm not calling to tell you your last agent did it wrong — I don't know that. I'm calling because I looked at your listing history and I have a specific theory about what happened, and I'd rather give it to you for free than pitch you. Do you have two minutes for it?"

The theory has to be real and specific — the photos, the price band, the days on market against the comps. Give it whether or not they hire you. On expireds you're competing against ten people who all opened with a version of "your agent failed you," and the seller is already sick of it by the time you dial.

ND + MN

Telling a client something they don't want to hear

The whole job, eventually.

"I'm going to tell you something you're not going to like, and I'd rather you hear it from me than find it out in six weeks. … That's my honest read. It's your house and your decision, and if you want to go the other way I'll run it hard and I won't sulk about it. But you hired me for the honest read, so there it is."

Say it once, clearly, then genuinely let it go. Agents who deliver hard advice and then keep relitigating it become exhausting and get ignored. Agents who deliver it once, document it, and execute the client's decision cheerfully are the ones who get called back for the next three houses.

When to walk

The expensive judgment
ND + MN

Listings we decline

A seller who won't price within a defensible range and won't sign a reduction schedule. A seller who instructs you to conceal a defect. A seller who won't sign the disclosure. A property where the title picture is unresolved and nobody will engage an attorney. A seller who wants to dictate who may buy.

An overpriced listing costs more than the commission you didn't earn. It costs weeks of your time, it makes your other sellers wonder why your listings sit, and it teaches the market that a TruHome sign means "not really for sale." Say no early and warmly — half of them call back in two months at your number.

ND + MN

Clients we end representation with

Anyone who asks you to do something you'd have to hide. Anyone who won't be truthful with a lender. Anyone who directs discrimination. Anyone abusive to staff, tenants, or the other side. Terminate through the broker, in writing, per the agreement's cancellation terms — never by going quiet.

Ghosting a client you've decided to quit is how a bad relationship becomes a formal complaint. Written termination, clean file, brief and unemotional. You never have to explain a professionally worded exit; you always have to explain silence.

ND + MN

Deals we advise killing

When an inspection reveals structural or environmental problems the buyer can't afford to absorb. When a seller's disclosure and the inspection contradict each other. When title won't clear on any workable timeline. When the buyer's financing has changed and the lender is being vague. When you can see the client is being pushed past what they can carry.

The hardest version is the client who wants it anyway. You give your recommendation clearly, once, in writing. Then it's their decision and you execute it. Documenting the advice protects them and you — and a year later, that email is the reason they still trust you.

ND + MN

When you don't know, stop

Unfamiliar property type, an out-of-state party, a contract for deed, a 1031 exchange, a trust or estate, a commercial lease clause, anything where you're improvising. Stop and ask the broker. Same day.

Nobody has ever been disciplined for asking. Every serious problem in this business started with someone deciding they could probably figure it out on their own before Friday.
